Opening summary (spoken, ~2 minutes, one speaker)

TL;DR: Three papers, two protections, one equation, four results, three simulation tests. Say it like you're explaining it to a friend. Learn it as the five steps below, not word for word.

The handout asks the opening to cover three things, each with the math and the simulations: (1) our questions and what prior work did, (2) what we did beyond it, and (3) what we found. The marginal tags [1] [2] [3] show where each one is covered.

The 2-minute opening

[1] Our paper asks when supply networks collapse suddenly, and how two protections firms use, buying each input from several suppliers and spreading them across regions, change that breaking point.

We built on three papers. Elliott, Golub and Leduc model supply chains as deep random trees. When firms need several inputs, output doesn't fade gradually; it falls off a cliff once supplier relationships get too weak. Carvalho and coauthors measure how the 2011 Japan earthquake spread through real supplier links and see smooth, fading spillovers. Dorogovtsev, Goltsev and Mendes show that k-cores of random graphs vanish abruptly, for the same mathematical reason. What none of them has is a firm that needs more than one working supplier per input, or a disaster that knocks out a whole region at once. So we asked three questions. Does needing two suppliers cause a cliff by itself? Is it safer to spread suppliers out or keep them together? And when a region is hit, does its number of firms matter more, or its supply?

[2] We extended the EGL model so that an input counts as covered only if at least k of its suppliers work, and a disaster shuts down every firm in a region. Reliability is still the largest fixed point of a single equation, so we could prove results.

[3] The math gives four results. Collapse is sudden whenever the number of suppliers needed times the number of inputs is at least two. With one input, spread and co-located suppliers break at the same shock. When one supplier is enough, spreading never hurts. But when firms need two, splitting suppliers into two regional pairs can be the worst option, because a firm that loses one pair is left barely sourced. In simulations of 420,000-firm networks, the thresholds matched the theory, the two-supplier networks split sharply into survivors and collapses, and hitting one supply-heavy region did far more damage than hitting many random ones.

Our work, explained

TL;DR: We took EGL's supply-tree model and added two real-world features: firms need k working suppliers per input, and disasters knock out whole regions. Reliability is still the largest fixed point of one equation, so we proved four results about when collapse is sudden and which supplier geography is safest. Then we checked them on simulated networks of 420,000 firms.

Notation is in 05-cheatsheet.md. The plots were computed from our own code/model.py by tools/make_prep_figures.py.

The whole story in one picture

1. The model

TL;DR: EGL's layered supply tree, plus two changes: k-of-n sourcing and regional shocks with c supplier clusters.

ELI5: Every bakery needs m ingredients. For each ingredient it has n possible suppliers one layer down, and each deal works with chance x. In EGL a bakery is fine if one supplier per ingredient comes through. In our version it needs k of them, for example because one supplier can't deliver enough. Also, storms hit whole towns: when a town is hit, every bakery in it closes. If a bakery's suppliers for an ingredient all live in the same town, one storm can wipe them all out at once.

Exactly what we assume:

Piece Meaning
Layers Firms sit in layers of a deep tree; each buys from the layer below (EGL's setup).
m, n, x m essential inputs, n potential suppliers per input, each relationship works with probability x.
k-of-n An input is covered only if at least k of its n suppliers are usable (the supplier works and the relationship works). EGL is k = 1.
Regional shock φ Each region is hit independently with probability φ, and a hit shuts down every firm in it. s = 1 − φ.
Clusters c A firm's n suppliers for an input form c clusters of n/c. A cluster shares a region; different clusters are in different regions. With n = 4: spread c = 4, pairs c = 2, co-located c = 1.

One firm's survival check:

The equation (paper eq. 2): let r be the chance that a firm works given that its own region survived. Then

r = F(r) = (P[an input is covered])^m

The coverage probability depends on geography (S_k(N, p) = chance that at least k of N suppliers are usable):

Geography Coverage probability Why
Spread (c = n) S_k(n, s·x·r) each supplier survives independently
Co-located (c = 1) s · S_k(n, x·r) all n survive together, or none do
Two pairs (n = 4) s²·S_k(4, xr) + 2sφ·S_k(2, xr) both pairs survive, or exactly one does

Reliability r* = the largest solution of r = F(r), as in EGL. The tolerable shock φ* = the largest φ with r* > 0.

DGM link: with m = 1 and spread suppliers, this equation has the same form as DGM's equation for the (k+1)-core. A core vertex needs k neighbors besides the one it was reached from, just as our firm needs k suppliers. So we expected a jump once k ≥ 2.

2. Why a cliff can happen: the fixed point picture

TL;DR: Reliability is where the S-shaped curve F(r) crosses the diagonal. A bigger shock lowers the curve. The high crossing disappears at a tangency, and reliability jumps to zero instead of sliding down.

ELI5: Imagine asking "if every supplier works with chance r, how likely am I to work?" The answer F(r) feeds back into the next layer. The network settles where the answer equals the question. If the curve dips under the diagonal everywhere, the only place left to settle is zero.

3. Claim 1: collapse is abrupt iff k·m ≥ 2

TL;DR: Needing two of anything (two inputs, or two suppliers for one input) creates a cliff. Only k = m = 1 fades gradually.

ELI5: If you need just one thing from one supplier, losing a little reliability costs a little. If you need two things, a small drop in supplier reliability hits you twice. Below some point the feedback can't sustain any small positive reliability, so the network has to jump from "pretty good" straight to "zero".

What we showed (the proof idea, which you should be able to say out loud): - The chance that at least k suppliers are usable is at most C(n,k)·p^k (add up over every group of k suppliers). - So F(r) ≤ K·r^(km) ≤ K·r² when km ≥ 2. For small r, F(r) < r: no small positive fixed point exists. - Reliability is therefore either 0 or at least 1/K. Since it moves monotonically with x and φ, it must jump. - If k = m = 1, then F(r) ≈ n·s·x·r for small r: a branching process with mean n·s·x (course §21.8), which grows continuously from 0.

What's new: for m ≥ 2 this is EGL's precipice. m = 1 with k ≥ 2 is new: one input with two required suppliers is already enough for a cliff.

How we checked it: in simulations with no shock (validation, E1), the k = 2, m = 1 networks drop from about 0.74 to 0 at x ≈ 0.725, matching the theory.

4. Claim 2: spread versus co-located, in closed form

TL;DR: (a) Spreading suppliers out is exactly like weakening every relationship from x to (1−φ)x, so φ*_spread = 1 − x_crit/x. (b) With one input, spread and co-located break at the same shock. With m inputs, φ*_co = 1 − (1 − φ*_spread)^(1/m), which is smaller.

ELI5: Spread suppliers each get hit separately, which just looks like every deal being a bit less reliable. Co-located suppliers are all-or-nothing. With one input those two kinds of risk balance out exactly at the breaking point. With several inputs, co-location gives each input its own chance of being wiped out, so the firm is hit m times over.

What we showed: write G(u) = [S_k(n, x·u)]^m. Spread gives r = G(s·r); multiplying by s and setting u = s·r gives u = s·G(u). Co-located gives r = s^m·G(r). Both are u = a·G(u), with a = s for spread and a = s^m for co-located. A positive solution exists exactly when a ≥ some cutoff a* that depends only on G. When m = 1 the two constants are the same.

Magnitudes to say in words: for m = 2, n = 4, x = 0.9, co-location roughly halves the tolerable shock. Needing k = 2 instead of k = 1 shrinks it more than fivefold.

5. Claim 3: with k = 1, splitting clusters never hurts

TL;DR: If one working supplier is enough, splitting each regional cluster into smaller ones (c → c′ with c dividing c′) never lowers reliability or the tolerable shock.

ELI5: If you only need one supplier, more separate "bets" is always better than fewer big bets.

What we showed (Jensen's inequality): with k = 1, an input fails only if no supplier is usable. Let q = 1 − x·r. A cluster of b suppliers gives nothing with probability φ + s·q^b (it's hit, or it survives but all b fail). Splitting it into two halves replaces that with (φ + s·q^(b/2))². Since y ↦ y² is convex, (φ·1 + s·t)² ≤ φ·1² + s·t² with t = q^(b/2). So each input fails less often, F goes up, and so does its largest fixed point.

6. Example 1: with k = 2, two pairs can be the worst arrangement

TL;DR: With n = 4, k = 2, m = 1, x = 0.9: co-located and spread suppliers both break at φ* ≈ 0.195, but two regional pairs break earlier, at ≈ 0.171. Claim 3 does not extend to k ≥ 2.

ELI5: Losing one of your two towns doesn't kill you, but it leaves you with exactly the two suppliers you need, and both have to come through. Near the breaking point, suppliers only come through about 60% of the time, so "both must work" is a bad bet. Co-located firms are either untouched (four suppliers, plenty of slack) or wiped out. Near the edge, the "barely sourced" middle state is worse.

Interesting detail in the plot: for small shocks, pairs actually have higher reliability than co-located suppliers, yet they break first. Safety in normal times and safety at the edge are different things.

How general: in a grid search (even n ≤ 8, m ≤ 3, six values of x), reversals like this appeared in 72 of the 109 settings with k ≥ 2 where some geography tolerates a shock, and in none with k = 1. The simulations also show a similar reversal for n = 6, k = 3.

DGM link: the barely sourced firms are like DGM's vertices with exactly k core neighbors. Remove one more and they fall out.

Mechanism link (prior work): coverage S_k is S-shaped (convex, then concave) in the number of surviving suppliers. Bimpikis–Fearing–Tahbaz-Salehi get a similar backfire from S-shaped production. To our knowledge, ours is the first network-wide case where regional pairs are worst.

7. The simulations

TL;DR: The claims are about infinitely deep trees, so we built finite layered networks and checked every claim. Theory and simulation agree closely, and the simulations also answer Q3.

Setup: a bottom layer plus 20 layers above it, 20,000 firms each (420,000 total), placed in 200 regions. Mostly n = 4 and x = 0.9 (above x_crit in every case, so collapse comes from the shock). Each firm picks random suppliers in the layer below following its cluster pattern. Each run is compared with 20 steps of the equation, evaluated at the share of regions actually hit in that run.

Experiment (repo label) Question What we found (in words)
Validation (E1) Do simulations match the equation? Yes: over 3,360 runs the average gap from the 20-layer theory is tiny (about 0.0025).
Bimodality (E2) Q1: is k = 2 really a cliff in finite networks? At the predicted threshold, k = 1 runs all fade low; k = 2 runs split into two groups, collapsed or healthy. Which group a run lands in tracks how many regions happened to be hit.
Thresholds (E3, E5) Q2: spread vs pairs vs co-located Using 2,000 regions and bisection on the exact number hit, all 20 simulated thresholds sit right on the 20-layer theory. Co-location hurts with two inputs; pairs break first with one input and k = 2; similar reversal for n = 6, k = 3.
Hub regions (E4) Q3: firm count vs supply share Popular suppliers barely matter (about 0.03). But one region holding 11% of supply and only 0.5% of firms collapses a k = 2 network on its own, while 10 random regions (5% of firms) do so only rarely. The theory fits when the shock is measured as the share of supplier clusters hit.
k-core check (E6) Is our pruning code right? On 100,000-vertex random graphs, every k-core (k = 2 … 7) vanishes within about 0.01 of DGM's thresholds.

Why two theory columns in the table (infinite vs 20 layers)? A finite network has less depth for failures to build up, so its thresholds sit a bit higher than the infinite-depth ones. We compare simulations with the 20-layer theory, and they match it.

9. Limitations and next steps (be ready to say these yourself)

  • Trees, not real networks: no cycles, no hubs in the theory (we test hubs only in simulation).
  • Fixed geography: firms don't choose k, n or c. Next: let firms choose how many suppliers and regions to use, at a cost, compute equilibria, and compare with a planner. We expect under-diversification and, with k = 2, sometimes pairs.
  • No data test yet. Next: use OECD inter-country input–output tables (countries as regions) around the 2011 Thailand floods and check whether output losses jump once an industry's input share from the hit region passes some level.
  • Smaller open questions: exactly when pairs are worst; what cycles change; protecting firms on a budget (a reverse influence maximization, as in Kempe–Kleinberg–Tardos).

EGL: Elliott, Golub & Leduc (2022), "Supply Network Formation and Fragility"

American Economic Review 112(8), 2701–2747. Our starting point and the model we extend.

TL;DR: In a deep supply network where firms need several inputs, reliability doesn't decline smoothly. It falls off a cliff ("precipice") once supplier relationships get too weak. Worse, when firms pay to strengthen their own relationships, the market settles right next to that cliff, so a tiny shock can wipe out production. A planner would never choose that.

ELI5

A car factory needs an engine and tires and glass. Each part has a few possible suppliers, and each supplier needs its own parts from suppliers below it, and so on down the chain. If every deal is quite reliable, almost everything gets built. But as deals get a little less reliable, failures compound across layers and across the parts you need. Past a certain point the whole chain can't keep itself going, and output drops from "most things work" to "almost nothing works" all at once. Each firm only pays to protect itself, not its customers, so firms tend to stop investing just short of safety, right on the edge.

What they did

Piece Their setup
Network Firms in layers of a deep random tree: each product needs m essential inputs, each with n potential suppliers in the layer below.
Failure Each supplier relationship works independently with probability x (relationship strength).
Rule A firm works if every input has at least one working relationship with a working supplier.
Reliability R_x(r) = (1 − (1 − x·r)^n)^m. In a deep network, reliability is the largest fixed point of R_x.
Investment game Each firm pays a cost to raise its own x. The paper compares the equilibrium with a social planner.

Key results

  1. Precipice (their Proposition 1): for m ≥ 2 (and n ≥ 2) there is a critical strength x_crit. Below it reliability is 0; just above it reliability is clearly positive, and the curve is nearly vertical there. For m = 1 the drop is continuous (a branching process).
  2. Equilibrium bunches at the edge (their Theorem 1, proved for m ≥ 3): for a whole range of investment costs, equilibrium strength sits within a hair of x_crit. Reliability is so steep there that each firm's best response lands on the steep part.
  3. Inefficiency: a planner never puts the network near x_crit, because near the threshold the social value of investing a bit more is enormous.
  4. Fragility: in that critical regime, a tiny drop in baseline relationship quality (a systemic shock) collapses production. Multisourcing protects against independent failures but not against correlated ones.

How we used it, and how we differ

  • Same skeleton: we keep the deep layered tree, m, n, x, and "reliability = largest fixed point".
  • Change 1, k-of-n sourcing: EGL is the special case k = 1. We require k usable suppliers per input.
  • Change 2, regional shocks: EGL's failures are independent, and their only aggregate shock is a uniform drop in x. We add disasters that hit whole regions, so co-located suppliers fail together.
  • Result link: Claim 1 says the cliff appears iff k·m ≥ 2. That generalizes their Proposition 1 (m ≥ 2) and adds the new case m = 1, k ≥ 2.
  • Claim 2(a) uses their machinery directly: spread suppliers turn a regional shock into an EGL-style weakening x → (1−φ)x, so φ*_spread = 1 − x_crit/x.

Weaknesses we named (from our paper)

  1. Relationship failures are independent; the only aggregate shock is a uniform drop in x. Real disasters are regional. (We fix this.)
  2. One working supplier always suffices, which ignores capacity limits. (We fix this.)
  3. The tree has no hubs or cycles, the equilibrium theorem is proved only for m ≥ 3, and x_crit can't be observed in data. (We test hubs in simulation.)

Course connections

  • The failure rule is a threshold rule (Granovetter; Easley–Kleinberg §19.2), run in reverse: a firm drops out when, for some input, none of its suppliers remains.
  • With m = 1 the recursion is the survival probability of a branching process (E–K §21.8).
  • With m ≥ 2 it resembles a complex contagion (Centola–Macy): you need several things to go right.
  • In the spirit of Kleinberg (2007), firms' own choices set how close the network sits to its threshold.

Questions staff might ask

  • Why does needing two inputs create a jump when one input doesn't? With m = 1 the recursion is linear near 0 (a branching process), so reliability grows from 0 smoothly. With m ≥ 2, F(r) is roughly r² near 0, so there is no small positive fixed point and reliability must jump.
  • Why do firms end up near the cliff? Reliability is extremely steep near x_crit, and firms don't internalize their customers' benefit, so best responses bunch there for a range of costs. A planner would invest more.
  • What would you test empirically? The cliff itself: losses should jump once exposure crosses a threshold. That is our OECD / Thailand-floods plan.

CNST: Carvalho, Nirei, Saito & Tahbaz-Salehi (2021), "Supply Chain Disruptions: Evidence from the Great East Japan Earthquake"

Quarterly Journal of Economics 136(2), 1255–1321. The leading empirical study of shocks spreading through supply chains, and the shock it studies is regional.

TL;DR: After the 2011 earthquake and tsunami, firms whose suppliers or customers were in the disaster area grew several percentage points more slowly than similar firms that weren't linked to it, and the effect faded with each step away in the network. A calibrated model says these network links roughly doubled the national GDP hit (0.47 pp of growth, versus 0.21 pp if links out of the region had been cut).

ELI5

The tsunami hit a few prefectures holding only about 5% of Japan's output. On its own that should barely dent the national economy. But firms elsewhere bought parts from, or sold to, firms in that area. Those firms slowed down too, and so did their partners, a little less each step away. By comparing linked firms with unlinked look-alikes (difference-in-differences), the authors measure how far the damage traveled.

What they did

Piece Their setup
Data Proprietary Tokyo Shoko Research supplier–customer links. The panel has 750,237 firms, about 5 suppliers each, with heavy-tailed degrees.
Shock March 2011 earthquake and tsunami. The four affected prefectures hold 4.6% of output; their own losses account for only about 0.1 pp of national growth.
Design Difference-in-differences: sales growth of firms at network distance 1, 2, 3, 4 from disaster-area firms, against firms 5+ links away.
Robustness A placebo network with randomly rewired links shows no effect. Tsunami-only definitions, single-plant firms and power-grid splits give consistent results.
Model General equilibrium with nested CES production. Intermediates are substitutable with each other, and primary factors and intermediates are complements.

Key results

  1. Firms with a supplier in the disaster area: −3.8 pp sales growth. With a customer there: −3.1 pp.
  2. The effects shrink with network distance, but are still visible several links away.
  3. The calibrated model attributes 0.47 pp of lost GDP growth to the disaster, versus 0.21 pp without links out of the region.

How we used it, and how we differ

  • Motivation: real disasters are regional, and supply links really do carry shocks. That motivates our regional-shock model.
  • Calibration hint: firms have about five suppliers, so we mostly use n = 4 per input.
  • Hubs: CNST's heavy-tailed degrees motivate our hub-region and popular-supplier simulations (Q3).
  • The puzzle we resolve: CNST see smooth propagation, while EGL and we predict cliffs. These are compatible for three reasons: 1. Their model uses smooth CES substitution and firms never shut down, so it cannot produce a jump. 2. Their sample requires sales in every year 2010–2012, which would exclude firms that exited, the outcome most telling of collapse. 3. Even in EGL's model, a network far above its tipping point absorbs shocks smoothly.

Weaknesses we named (from our paper)

  1. Production substitutes smoothly and firms never shut down, so output falls smoothly and can't show a threshold.
  2. Exposure is network distance to the disaster area, which treats a firm with 1 of 10 suppliers there the same as one with 10 of 10.
  3. The sample requires sales every year, which would drop exits.

Our model suggests measuring exposure as the share of a firm's suppliers for each input in the hit region, and predicts a sharp drop once fewer than k suppliers survive. CNST's data is proprietary, so our plan is a first aggregate test on public OECD inter-country input–output tables around the 2011 Thailand floods (countries as regions). Per-input exposure and exit would need firm-level data.

Course connections

  • A clean causal measurement of contagion on a real network.
  • Their model spreads a shock through a damped sum over supply paths, like the walk sums behind PageRank (E–K §14.6).

Questions staff might ask

  • Doesn't CNST's smooth evidence contradict your cliffs? No. Their model can't make a jump, their sample drops exits, and a network far from its tipping point looks smooth anyway. Our model predicts where to look for a jump: firms left with fewer than k suppliers for some input.
  • How would you test your model with data like theirs? Measure per-input supplier exposure, and look for a kink once fewer than k survive, plus more exits among the most exposed.
  • Why does the placebo matter? It shows the effect travels along actual links, not just through geography or industry.

DGM: Dorogovtsev, Goltsev & Mendes (2006), "k-Core Organization of Complex Networks"

Physical Review Letters 96, 040601. The mathematical cousin of our model: a structure that collapses abruptly under random damage.

TL;DR: The k-core is what's left after you repeatedly delete every vertex with fewer than k neighbors. On random graphs, the k-core shrinks smoothly for k = 2, but for k ≥ 3 it vanishes with a jump as vertices are randomly removed (a "hybrid" transition: a jump plus a square-root singularity). The same fixed-point math is behind our cliff.

ELI5

Think of a friend group where you only stay if at least k of your friends stay. Remove a few people at random, and anyone left with too few friends leaves too, which can make their friends leave. With k = 2 the group just thins out gradually. With k = 3 or more, it can look fine and then unravel all at once: many members are hanging on with exactly k friends, so one more departure sets off an avalanche.

What they did

Piece Their setup
Graphs Random graphs with a given degree distribution (the configuration model). They look locally like trees, which allows a fixed-point analysis.
Damage Remove a random fraction Q = 1 − p of vertices.
Method Find the probability that following an edge leads into a big enough subtree. This gives a self-consistency (fixed-point) equation. For Erdős–Rényi graphs with mean degree z: u = p·P[Poisson(z·u) ≥ k−1].
Core size A vertex is in the k-core if at least k of its neighbors lead into such subtrees.

Key results

  1. k = 2: the core shrinks continuously, like the giant component (ordinary percolation).
  2. k ≥ 3: the core jumps to zero at a threshold, approached with a square-root singularity: a hybrid transition.
  3. Near the threshold, clusters of core vertices with exactly k core neighbors grow very large; their mean size diverges. (The same authors later call this set the corona.)
  4. Example (Erdős–Rényi, mean degree 10): the highest core disappears after only about 1.2% of vertices are removed, while the ordinary giant component survives until about 90% are gone. (Solving the equations ourselves, the 7-core vanishes at 1.25%.)
  5. Scale-free graphs with 2 < γ ≤ 3 have an infinite sequence of k-cores that are ultra-robust to random damage.

How we used it, and how we differ

  • Same math: with m = 1 and spread suppliers, our equation u = s·S_k(n, x·u) has the same form as DGM's u = p·P[Poisson(z·u) ≥ K−1]. Our k matches their K = k + 1 core: a core vertex needs k neighbors besides the one it was reached from, just as our firm needs k suppliers.
  • Prediction: so we expected a cliff once k ≥ 2, which Claim 1 proves (DGM's K ≥ 3 corresponds to our k ≥ 2).
  • Corona ↔ "barely sourced" firms: in Example 1, firms that lose one regional pair are left with exactly k suppliers, like DGM's vertices with exactly k core neighbors. They are the ones that tip the network.
  • Our check of their result: our own pruning code (code/kcore.py), run on simulated 100,000-vertex Erdős–Rényi graphs, finds every k-core (k = 2 … 7) vanishing within about 0.01 of DGM's thresholds. This also validates our simulation tooling.

Weaknesses we named (from our paper)

  • Mean-field: it ignores short cycles and degree correlations.
  • Damage is random and independent.
  • Every vertex has the same threshold, and links are undirected.
  • A supply-network version needs directed links, firm-specific thresholds, and correlated damage. Our model adds directed links and correlated (regional) damage.

Course connections

  • Uses the configuration model and branching / tree arguments from the Random Graphs part of the course.
  • Pruning a core is itself a threshold cascade: a vertex "fails" when too few neighbors remain.

Questions staff might ask

  • Why exactly is K = k + 1? In a tree-like graph you reach a vertex through one edge. To stay in the K-core it needs K − 1 other neighbors that also stay. Our firm needs k suppliers below it, so k = K − 1.
  • Why does k = 2 behave differently from k ≥ 3? For the 2-core, the equation is linear near zero (like a branching process) and the core grows from 0. For k ≥ 3, the right-hand side is at least quadratic near 0, so no small solution exists and the core must jump. That is the same argument as our Claim 1.
  • What's the "corona" and why do you mention it? It's the set of core vertices hanging on with exactly k neighbors; it explains the avalanche. Our barely sourced firms play the same role in Example 1.

One-sentence positioning (memorize this one):

Papachristou et al. study supply cascades with percolation, but one supplier suffices and geography is just a correlation knob. The three-tier papers show that shared suppliers correlate failures and that dual sourcing can backfire with S-shaped production. We put explicit regions into a full cascade, solve the extremes in closed form, and show that needing k ≥ 2 suppliers alone creates the S-shape. By a mechanism like Bimpikis et al.'s, this gives, to our knowledge, the first network-wide case where regional pairs are worst.

Papachristou, Rahimian & Azadegan, "Structural Measures of Resilience for Supply Chains" (arXiv 2303.12660, v4 2026)

TL;DR: They define a network's resilience as the largest supplier failure rate it can sustain while keeping aggregate production. They compute it with percolation and branching processes, find which structures are fragile, and extend the analysis to correlated failures such as geographic ones.

ELI5: How many suppliers can randomly break before the factory output collapses? Some network shapes ("top hat": many raw materials, very centralized) break easily; others ("rolling pin": controlled width, sparse) absorb shocks. If suppliers tend to fail together, diversifying helps less.

How we differ: in their model one surviving supplier per input is enough, and regions enter only as a correlation parameter. We model regions explicitly and require k suppliers, and that is exactly when "spread across two regions" can backfire (Example 1).

Ang, Iancu & Swinney (2017), "Disruption Risk and Optimal Sourcing in Multitier Supply Networks"

Management Science 63(8), 2397–2419.

TL;DR: If two tier-1 suppliers secretly rely on the same tier-2 supplier, their disruptions coincide. The manufacturer then relies less on direct protection (inventory, multisourcing) and more on getting tier-1 suppliers to reduce their own risk. It always prefers less overlap, but tier-1 suppliers may prefer more, which creates a coordination problem that penalty contracts can ease.

ELI5: You buy from two bakeries to be safe, but both buy flour from the same mill. One mill fire and both bakeries fail. Your "backup" wasn't a backup.

How we differ: they analyze one small chain and contracts. We look at a whole deep network and ask which geographic split is safest when firms need k suppliers.

Bimpikis, Fearing & Tahbaz-Salehi (2018), "Multisourcing and Miscoordination in Supply Chain Networks"

Operations Research 66(4), 1023–1039. Closest mechanism to our Example 1.

TL;DR: Two tier-1 suppliers each decide whether to buy from one fabricator or split between two. Splitting (dual sourcing) lowers each supplier's own risk but correlates their shortfalls. If production is S-shaped (convex at low input, concave above), simultaneous shortfalls are very costly, so suppliers may dual-source when the manufacturer would prefer they didn't. Diversification can be excessive.

ELI5: Below a minimum amount of material a supplier can barely produce anything (the convex part). If everyone hedges the same way, everyone ends up short at the same time, and that is worse for the buyer than some suppliers being fully fine and others fully out.

How we relate: our coverage probability S_k (need ≥ k of the surviving suppliers) is S-shaped in the number of survivors whenever k ≥ 2, with no assumption about production. So the same kind of backfire appears, but at the level of a whole cascading network, and it is driven purely by the k-of-n requirement. We credit them for the mechanism and claim the network-wide version.

Course references we cite

Short cards. Each one says what the work is, and where it shows up in our paper (the part staff care about).

Granovetter (1978), "Threshold Models of Collective Behavior" (AJS)

  • TL;DR: Each person joins (a riot, a fad) once enough others have joined. Their personal threshold decides when, and small changes in the threshold distribution can flip the outcome.
  • ELI5: "I'll dance if at least three people are already dancing."
  • In our paper: our failure rule is a threshold rule in reverse: a firm drops out when, for some input, fewer than k suppliers remain.

Easley & Kleinberg (2010), Networks, Crowds, and Markets: §19.2 (networked coordination / threshold q)

  • TL;DR: A node adopts a behavior once at least a fraction q of its neighbors have. This leads to cascades and to "blocking clusters".
  • In our paper: the same threshold logic (cited next to Granovetter).

Easley & Kleinberg §21.8 (branching processes, advanced material)

  • TL;DR: In a branching process, each individual has a random number of children. The population survives forever with positive probability iff the mean is above 1, and that survival probability grows continuously from 0.
  • In our paper: the k = m = 1 case of our recursion is a branching process with mean n·s·x. That is why that case fades smoothly (Claim 1's second half).

Easley & Kleinberg §14.6 (PageRank and random walks, advanced material)

  • TL;DR: PageRank can be written as a damped sum over all walks in the link graph.
  • In our paper: CNST's model spreads a shock through a damped sum over supply paths, the same structure.

Centola & Macy (2007), "Complex Contagions and the Weakness of Long Ties" (AJS)

  • TL;DR: Some behaviors need reinforcement from several contacts (complex contagion), not one (simple contagion). For those, long-range ties help less than clustered ones.
  • ELI5: You catch a cold from one person, but you join a protest only if several friends go.
  • In our paper: needing m ≥ 2 inputs (or k ≥ 2 suppliers) is like a complex contagion of failure: several things must go right, and that is what produces the cliff.

Kleinberg (2007), "Cascading Behavior in Networks: Algorithmic and Economic Issues" (in Algorithmic Game Theory)

  • TL;DR: A survey chapter on cascades: threshold and cascade models, when cascades spread, and the algorithmic and strategic questions around them.
  • In our paper: the new element in EGL, in this spirit, is that agents' own choices (investment in relationships) set how close the network sits to its threshold.

Kempe, Kleinberg & Tardos (2003), "Maximizing the Spread of Influence through a Social Network" (KDD)

  • TL;DR: Choose k seed nodes to maximize the spread of a cascade. Under standard cascade models the spread function is submodular, so greedy selection is a (1 − 1/e)-approximation.
  • In our paper: our open question, protecting firms on a budget, is a reverse influence-maximization problem (choose whom to protect so that the fewest fail).

A. Our questions and prior work

QA1. What question does your paper ask, in one sentence? ★

Short: When do supply networks collapse suddenly, and how do two protections, having several suppliers per input and spreading them across regions, move that breaking point? If pushed: Concretely: Q1, does needing k ≥ 2 working suppliers create a cliff even with one input? Q2, is it safer to spread suppliers or keep them together, and does that depend on k? Q3, when a region is hit, does its number of firms or its share of supply matter more?

QA2. Why did you pick these three papers? ★

Short: EGL gives the theory of sudden collapse, CNST gives the best evidence of how a regional shock spreads through real supply links, and DGM explains abrupt collapse in k-cores, which is the same math as our model. If pushed: None of the three is linked from the course page. EGL is the model we extend, CNST motivates the regional shock and our calibration (about five suppliers per firm), and DGM tells us what to expect when a node needs k neighbors.

QA3. What is EGL's "precipice"?

Short: When firms need several inputs, reliability doesn't decline gradually as relationships weaken; it jumps from a healthy level to zero at a critical relationship strength. If pushed: Reliability is the largest fixed point of R_x(r) = (1 − (1 − xr)^n)^m. For m ≥ 2 that curve is S-shaped, so as x falls the high crossing disappears at a tangency. They also show that firms investing in their own relationships end up just above that point, and a planner never would.

QA4. What does CNST find, and how do they know it's causal?

Short: Firms linked to the 2011 disaster area, as suppliers or customers, grew several points more slowly, and the effect faded with network distance. A placebo network with randomly rewired links shows no effect. If pushed: Difference-in-differences on roughly three-quarters of a million Japanese firms, comparing firms 1–4 links from the disaster area with firms 5+ links away. Their calibrated model says network links roughly doubled the GDP hit.

QA5. What's a k-core, and why is DGM relevant to supply chains?

Short: It's what remains after repeatedly deleting nodes with fewer than k neighbors. For k ≥ 3 it vanishes with a jump under random damage, and our equation with one input is the same equation as DGM's. If pushed: Our k corresponds to their K = k + 1 core: a core vertex needs k neighbors besides the one it was reached from, as our firm needs k suppliers below it. So DGM predicted our cliff for k ≥ 2.

QA6. Hasn't someone already studied geographic diversification in supply networks? ★

Short: Yes, partly. Papachristou et al. study supply cascades with percolation and correlated failures, but one supplier suffices and regions are just a correlation parameter. Ang–Iancu–Swinney and Bimpikis–Fearing–Tahbaz-Salehi study three-tier chains. If pushed: Bimpikis et al. show dual sourcing can backfire with S-shaped production. We get a similar backfire from the k-of-n rule alone, in a full cascading network with explicit regions. To our knowledge, regional pairs being worst network-wide is new, and so is the closed form for spread versus co-located.

QA7. What's missing from the prior literature that you add?

Short: Two things: firms needing more than one working supplier per input, and disasters that knock out whole regions so that co-located suppliers fail together. If pushed: EGL and DGM assume independent failures; CNST's model has no backup suppliers. Real firms dual-source because of capacity or policy, and real disasters are regional.

B. What we did

QB1. Describe your model in plain words. ★

Short: EGL's layered supply tree, where each firm needs m inputs and has n possible suppliers per input. We add that an input is only covered if at least k suppliers come through, and that disasters hit whole regions, where a firm's suppliers sit in c regional clusters. If pushed: A supplier is usable if it works and the relationship works (probability x). Each region is hit with probability φ. Reliability r is the chance a firm works given that its own region survived, and it satisfies r = F(r) = (coverage)^m, where coverage depends on the geography.

QB2. What does k mean, and why would a firm need more than one supplier?

Short: k is how many suppliers for an input must come through. Firms need more than one because of capacity limits (one supplier can't cover all demand) or deliberate dual-sourcing policies. If pushed: EGL is the special case k = 1. Raising k is a different kind of fragility from raising m: you need more of the same input, not more inputs.

QB3. How do you model a regional disaster, and what is c?

Short: Each region is hit independently with probability φ, which shuts every firm in it. A firm's n suppliers for an input form c clusters that each share a region: c = n is spread, c = 1 is co-located, and with n = 4, c = 2 is two pairs. If pushed: Different clusters are (almost always) in different regions, so cluster survivals are independent.

QB4. Why keep the tree structure?

Short: Because it gives one clean fixed-point equation we can prove things about, the same reason EGL and DGM use it. If pushed: Random sparse graphs look locally tree-like, so the tree approximation is the standard mean-field analysis. Cycles are a known limitation, and we list them as future work.

QB5. What is reliability r*, and why the largest fixed point?

Short: It's the chance a firm in a deep network works (given its own region is fine). It's the largest solution of r = F(r), because starting from working suppliers at the bottom (r = 1) and applying F layer by layer converges to the largest solution. If pushed: F is increasing, so iterating from 1 gives a decreasing sequence that stops at the first crossing it meets, which is the largest one. Zero is always a solution, so a positive one is what we care about.

QB6. Why both math and simulations?

Short: The math is about infinitely deep trees. The simulations check that the same thresholds and behaviors show up in finite networks with real randomness, and they let us test things the math doesn't cover, like hub regions. If pushed: We compare each run with 20 steps of the equation at the share of regions actually hit, and the two agree closely.

QB7. How did you use AI, and what did you do yourselves?

Short: We used Claude to help edit drafts, write and test the simulation code, check proofs numerically, and format the paper. We chose the questions, reviewed the analysis, reran the code and checked every number. (Make this true and specific for you.) If pushed: Be ready to explain any result from scratch. That's the point of this conversation.

C. What we found

QC1. What are your main findings? ★

Short: Needing two of anything makes collapse sudden; with one input, spread and co-located suppliers break at the same shock; with one required supplier, spreading never hurts; but with two required, two regional pairs can be the worst arrangement. Simulations confirm all of it. If pushed: And for Q3: a region's share of supply matters much more than how many firms it holds.

QC2. What's the most surprising result? ★

Short: That splitting suppliers across two regions can be worse than both putting them all in one region and spreading them all out, when firms need two suppliers. If pushed: A firm that loses one pair is left with exactly two suppliers and needs both. Near the tipping point each works only about 60% of the time, so that "barely sourced" state is worse than the all-or-nothing outcome of co-location.

QC3. So is spreading suppliers out always better?

Short: Fully spreading is never worse than co-locating (they tie with one input, and spreading wins with more). Partial spreading is the risky one: with k = 1 any finer split helps, but with k ≥ 2 an intermediate split like two pairs can be worst. If pushed: In our grid search the reversal showed up in 72 of the 109 settings with k ≥ 2 where some geography survives a shock, and never with k = 1. It's common, but it doesn't hold everywhere.

QC4. Does a region's number of firms or its share of supply matter more?

Short: Supply share, by far. One region holding a big share of supply relationships but very few firms can collapse a k = 2 network on its own, while hitting many random regions rarely does. If pushed: The theory matches once the shock is measured as the share of supplier clusters hit, not the share of firms. Heavy-tailed supplier popularity on its own barely matters, because a popular supplier is no more likely to fail.

QC5. What's the practical takeaway?

Short: Simple "diversify across two regions" advice can backfire when firms need several suppliers per input. Exposure should be measured per input, by the share of suppliers in a region, not by distance to the disaster. If pushed: It also says where cliffs should show up in data: firms left with fewer than k suppliers for some input.

QC6. How robust is the pairs result?

Short: It's exact in the model: we derive the coverage difference in closed form and see why its sign flips at p = 2/3. The simulations separate the thresholds clearly. But it isn't universal: it depends on n, k, m and x. If pushed: For example, with two inputs, n = 4 and k = 2, the ordering is monotone (co-located < pairs < spread). We'd like a clean rule for when pairs are worst; that's an open question.

QC7. How do you reconcile CNST's smooth evidence with your cliffs?

Short: They're compatible. CNST's model has smooth substitution and no shutdowns, so it can't produce a jump; their sample requires sales every year, which excludes exits; and a network far from its tipping point absorbs shocks smoothly anyway. If pushed: Our model predicts where to look for a jump: per-input supplier exposure crossing the point where fewer than k survive.

D. Math

QD1. Sketch the proof that collapse is abrupt when k·m ≥ 2 (Claim 1). ★

Short: By a union bound, F(r) ≤ K·r^(km) ≤ K·r². So for small r, F(r) < r: there is no small positive fixed point. Reliability is either 0 or at least 1/K, and since it moves monotonically, it has to jump. If pushed: K = C(n,k)^m. The union bound adds up the chance over every group of k suppliers that all of them are usable.

QD2. Why does k = m = 1 fade smoothly instead?

Short: Near zero F(r) ≈ n·s·x·r, which is a branching process with mean n·s·x. Its survival probability grows continuously from 0 once the mean passes 1. If pushed: That's E–K §21.8. The same as DGM's 2-core (ordinary percolation) behaving continuously.

QD3. Why do spread and co-located suppliers break at the same shock with one input? ★

Short: Both cases reduce to the same equation u = a·G(u). Only the constant in front differs: a = s for spread and a = s^m for co-located. With one input those are equal. If pushed: Spread: r = G(s·r); set u = s·r to get u = s·G(u). Co-located: r = s^m·G(r). A positive solution exists exactly when a is at least a cutoff a* that depends only on G. Reliability differs, but the breaking point doesn't.

QD4. Why does co-location hurt with several inputs?

Short: With co-located suppliers, each input has its own chance of being wiped out by its region, and the firm needs all m inputs, so it gets hit m times over. If pushed: Formally φ*_co = 1 − (1 − φ*_spread)^(1/m), which is smaller. For two inputs the tolerable shock roughly halves.

QD5. Why does spreading never hurt when k = 1 (Claim 3)?

Short: With k = 1 an input fails only if no supplier is usable, and splitting a cluster in two turns one big all-or-nothing bet into two smaller ones. By Jensen's inequality, that lowers the failure probability. If pushed: A cluster of b gives nothing with probability φ + s·q^b, with q = 1 − x·r. Halving it gives (φ + s·q^(b/2))², and convexity of y² gives (φ + s·t)² ≤ φ + s·t². So F goes up, and so does its largest fixed point.

QD6. Explain intuitively why pairs are worst. ★

Short: A paired firm often loses one pair and is left with exactly two suppliers, both of which must work. Near the tipping point suppliers are flaky, so that is a bad state. A co-located firm is either untouched with four suppliers or wiped out. If pushed: The coverage gap is s·φ·[2p² − S₂(4,p)], with p = x·r. The bracket is negative when p < 2/3, and at the pairs tipping point p is only about 0.6.

QD7. Where does the 2/3 come from?

Short: It's where 2p² equals S₂(4,p), the chance that at least 2 of 4 are usable. Below it, needing both of two is worse than needing two of four in an all-or-nothing region. If pushed: S₂(4,p) = 1 − (1−p)⁴ − 4p(1−p)³. Setting it equal to 2p² and solving gives p = 2/3 (besides p = 0).

QD8. What exactly is the connection to DGM's equation?

Short: With one input and spread suppliers, writing u = s·r gives u = s·S_k(n, x·u), the same form as DGM's u = p·P[Poisson(z·u) ≥ K − 1] with K = k + 1. If pushed: Both say: a node survives if enough of the branches below it survive. Binomial instead of Poisson, and directed instead of undirected.

QD9. What's the coverage formula for two pairs?

Short: s²·S_k(4, xr) + 2sφ·S_k(2, xr): both pairs survive (four candidates), or exactly one does (two candidates). If pushed: In general, with c clusters of size b: the sum over j surviving clusters of C(c,j)·s^j·φ^(c−j)·S_k(j·b, x·r).

QD10. Why is there a jump rather than a steep slope?

Short: Because the solution we're tracking, the high crossing of F with the diagonal, stops existing at a tangency. After that the only solution left is 0. If pushed: Just above the threshold reliability is clearly positive (for example, about 0.74 for the one-input, two-supplier case at x_crit), and just below it's zero.

E. Simulations

QE1. What did you simulate, and why? ★

Short: Finite layered networks, with a bottom layer plus 20 layers of 20,000 firms each, spread over regions. Each firm draws random suppliers according to its cluster pattern; we hit regions and let failures cascade upward. The goal was to check that the deep-tree theory holds in finite random networks and to test hub regions. If pushed: We compare each run with 20 steps of the equation at the share of regions actually hit in that run.

QE2. Why compare with "20-layer theory" and not the infinite-depth result?

Short: A finite network has less depth for failures to build up, so its thresholds sit a bit higher. Twenty steps of the equation is the right comparison for a 20-layer network, and the simulations match it. If pushed: The infinite-depth values are still the ones the claims are about; the gap between the two is a depth effect, not an error.

QE3. What does the bimodality experiment show? ★

Short: At the predicted threshold, k = 1 networks all fade to low reliability, while k = 2 networks split into two groups: collapsed or healthy, with little in between. That's what a cliff looks like in finite networks. If pushed: Which group a run lands in tracks how many regions happened to be hit in that run, just above or below the 20-layer threshold.

QE4. How did you measure tolerable shocks precisely?

Short: Using many small regions, we hit an exact number of them and found each network's tipping point by bisection. In a fixed network, hitting more regions can only hurt, so the tipping point is well defined. If pushed: 2,000 regions of 10 firms, 40 networks per setting, median tipping point with a bootstrap interval. All 20 settings' intervals contain the 20-layer theory.

QE5. What did the hub-region experiment show?

Short: A region that holds a large share of supply relationships but few firms can collapse a k = 2 network by itself, while hitting many random regions rarely does. What matters is supply share, not firm count. If pushed: The theory fits once φ is measured as the share of supplier clusters hit (supply-weighted). Making some suppliers far more popular than others barely changed reliability.

QE6. Why did you reproduce DGM's k-core thresholds?

Short: As a check on our tooling: our own pruning code on simulated 100,000-vertex random graphs finds every k-core vanishing close to DGM's predicted thresholds. If pushed: It also confirms the K = k + 1 analogy numerically, and the striking contrast: the 7-core disappears after removing only about 1.25% of vertices, while the giant component survives until about 90%.

QE7. How do you know your code is right?

Short: Three ways: the simulations match an independent theory computation; the code reproduces DGM's published k-core thresholds; and an automated test suite checks every claim against the numerical recursion. If pushed: We also made the results reproducible: fixed random seeds, and regenerating the figures gives identical files.

QE8. What is a "supply-weighted" shock?

Short: The share of supplier clusters whose region was hit, rather than the share of firms hit. With hub regions those two can be very different. If pushed: It's the quantity that enters the equation, because firms care about losing suppliers, not about how many firms live in the region.

F. Weaknesses and next steps

QF1. What's the biggest weakness of your model? ★

Short: Geography and sourcing are fixed: firms don't choose k, n or how to split suppliers across regions. And it's a tree, with no cycles. If pushed: We also assume region hits are independent and all firms share the same k. The next steps target the first point directly.

QF2. What would change with cycles or real network structure?

Short: Cycles create feedback and correlation between suppliers' failures that the tree ignores, so thresholds would likely shift. Whether the pairs reversal survives is open. If pushed: DGM's analysis has the same mean-field limitation. Simulating on real or clustered networks would be the test.

QF3. What's your final-project direction? ★

Short: Let firms choose how many suppliers and regions to use, at a cost, compute the equilibrium, and compare it with a planner's choice. If pushed: In EGL, equilibrium bunches near the cliff even though a planner never would. We expect firms to under-diversify, and with k = 2 sometimes to choose pairs, the worst split.

QF4. How would you test this with data?

Short: Use public OECD inter-country input–output tables, treating countries as regions, around the 2011 Thailand floods, and check whether industries' losses jump once their input share from the hit region passes some level, instead of growing in proportion. If pushed: Per-input supplier exposure and firm exit would need firm-level data like CNST's, which is proprietary.

QF5. Is k realistic? How would you measure it?

Short: It stands for capacity limits or dual-sourcing rules: if no single supplier can cover your volume, you need several. You'd estimate it from supplier shares or capacity per input. If pushed: In practice k would differ by firm and input; a heterogeneous-k version is a natural extension.

QF6. Why assume regions are hit independently?

Short: It's the simplest model of correlated failure: everything inside a region fails together, regions are independent. It isolates the effect of how suppliers are grouped. If pushed: If neighboring regions tend to be hit together, that effectively merges clusters (lowers c), which our framework can describe.

G. Course connections

QG1. How does this connect to the course? ★

Short: It's a cascade on a network with a threshold rule (a firm fails when too few suppliers remain), analyzed like branching processes and random-graph k-cores, and it shows the course's simple-versus-complex contagion distinction. If pushed: The k = m = 1 case is a branching process; needing several things is like a complex contagion; DGM's k-cores use the configuration model; protecting firms on a budget is like reverse influence maximization.

QG2. How is your failure rule related to threshold models?

Short: It's Granovetter's / Easley–Kleinberg's threshold rule run in reverse: instead of adopting when enough neighbors adopt, a firm fails when fewer than k of its suppliers for some input remain.

If pushed: And with m ≥ 2 it behaves like a complex contagion (Centola–Macy): several things must go right at once.

QG3. Where do branching processes come in?

Short: In the k = m = 1 case, each firm's survival depends on at least one of its suppliers surviving, which is exactly a branching process with mean n·s·x. That's why that case is smooth. If pushed: With k or m ≥ 2 the process is no longer "linear" near zero, which is what breaks continuity.

QG4. Where does influence maximization come in?

Short: As an open question: if you can protect a few firms, which ones minimize collapse? That's a reverse of choosing seeds to maximize spread (Kempe–Kleinberg–Tardos). If pushed: Our AND-type failure structure may break the submodularity that makes greedy work there, so it's a genuinely different problem.

H. "Explain it yourself" (checks that you understand without the paper)

QH1. Explain the cliff to someone with no math background. ★

Short: Each firm needs its suppliers, which need theirs, and so on. If needing one thing goes wrong a bit more often, things get a bit worse. But if you need two things, small problems compound, and past a point the chain can't sustain itself: it goes from mostly working to not working at all.

If pushed: Use your hands: a gentle slope for k = 1, a drop-off for k = 2.

QH2. Describe the fixed-point picture without drawing it.

Short: Plot "chance a firm works" against "chance its suppliers work". Reliability is where that S-shaped curve crosses the diagonal. A shock lowers the curve; when it only just touches the diagonal, one more push and the crossing is gone, so reliability falls to zero. If pushed: That touching point is the tangency at x_crit or φ*.

QH3. Walk me through one firm's survival check.

Short: Is my region hit? If not, for each input: which of my supplier clusters' regions survived? Of the surviving suppliers, how many work and have a working relationship? If at least k, that input is covered. I work if all inputs are covered. If pushed: In the equation that's coverage^m, with coverage a mix of binomial tails weighted by how many clusters survive.

QH4. What happens when you change k from 1 to 2, and why?

Short: The tolerable shock shrinks a lot, and the transition goes from smooth to a cliff even with one input, because one lucky supplier is no longer enough. You now need two. If pushed: In our two-input base case, the tolerable shock is more than five times smaller with k = 2.

I. Curveballs

QI1. Couldn't firms just hold inventory?

Short: Inventory isn't in the model. It would act as a buffer, roughly like stronger relationships, delaying collapse during short disruptions, but it doesn't remove the threshold structure. If pushed: It would be a natural extension: time-varying shocks with buffers. Ang–Iancu–Swinney treat inventory as a direct mitigation tool.

QI2. Why n = 4 and x = 0.9?

Short: n = 4 is close to the roughly five suppliers per firm in CNST's data, and x = 0.9 keeps the network above its critical strength in every case, so collapse comes from the regional shock and not from weak relationships. If pushed: We also show n = 6, k = 3, where a similar reversal appears.

QI3. Is the pairs result just a numerical artifact?

Short: No. There's an exact formula for the coverage difference and a clean reason for its sign (the 2/3 crossover). Simulations with tight intervals separate the pairs threshold from both extremes. If pushed: The grid scan also shows it is common for k ≥ 2 and absent for k = 1, exactly as Claim 3 says.

QI4. What result would prove you wrong?

Short: If real industries' losses grew smoothly with exposure even when per-input supplier exposure crossed the point where fewer than k survive, the threshold mechanism wouldn't be what drives real collapses. If pushed: That's the point of the Thailand-floods test.

QI5. What did you learn that you didn't expect?

Short: That "more diversified" isn't monotonically safer. Going from one region to two can hurt when firms need two suppliers, even though going all the way to four is fine. (Say it in your own words.)

If pushed: And that a regional shock on spread suppliers is mathematically just a weaker relationship strength, which made the closed form possible.

QI6. If you had another month, what would you do first?

Short: The endogenous-geography game, to see whether firms actually choose the fragile pairs arrangement in equilibrium. If pushed: Then a rule in (n, k, m, x) for exactly when pairs are worst, and a simulation on networks with cycles.

Vocabulary

Term Meaning
Precipice / cliff reliability jumps from a healthy value straight to 0 (a discontinuous, "first-order" transition).
Tipping point the x or φ where the jump happens (x_crit, φ*).
Fixed point where F(r) = r; reliability is the largest one. A tangency is where the high one disappears.
Branching process the k = m = 1 case: like a family tree that survives if each firm has > 1 working supplier on average. Smooth transition.
k-core what's left after repeatedly deleting vertices with fewer than k neighbors (DGM).
Corona core vertices with exactly k core neighbors; one more loss and they drop out (DGM 2006 describes them; the name is from the same authors' follow-up). Our "barely sourced" firms are the analogue.
Complex contagion Centola–Macy: adoption needs several contacts, not one. Our m ≥ 2 or k ≥ 2 failure rule is the reverse.
Threshold model Granovetter / Easley–Kleinberg §19.2: act when enough neighbors act. Our firms fail when too few suppliers remain.
Spread / pairs / co-located all suppliers in different regions / two regions with two suppliers each / all in one region.
Supply-weighted shock the share of supplier clusters hit, not the share of firms. This is what predicts collapse with hub regions.
Difference-in-differences CNST's method: compare how firms linked to the disaster area changed versus similar firms that weren't linked.

Ten takeaways (say them in words, no numbers needed)

  1. Needing two of anything (inputs or suppliers per input) turns gradual decline into a cliff (Claim 1).
  2. The new case is one input, two required suppliers: EGL needed several inputs for a cliff, and we don't.
  3. Spreading suppliers out is mathematically the same as weakening every relationship (Claim 2a).
  4. With one input, spread and co-located suppliers break at the same shock (Claim 2b).
  5. With several inputs, co-location breaks earlier, because each input can be wiped out separately.
  6. If one supplier is enough, splitting regions further never hurts (Claim 3, Jensen).
  7. If firms need two, two regional pairs can be the worst arrangement: losing one pair leaves firms barely sourced (Example 1).
  8. Partial diversification can backfire, so "split across two regions" is not automatically safe advice.
  9. Simulations on 420,000-firm networks match the theory; k = 2 networks split into survivors and collapses.
  10. What matters about a hit region is its share of supply, not its number of firms, so exposure should be measured by suppliers per input.
